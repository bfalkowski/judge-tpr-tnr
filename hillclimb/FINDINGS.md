# Hill climbing against an LLM judge: what the hillclimb skill sees and misses

Bryan Falkowski · October 2026 · Design: [`DESIGN.md`](DESIGN.md) · Every number: [`RESULTS.md`](RESULTS.md), generated by `analyze.py`

*Working notes, not yet a post.*

## Summary

The `claude-api` skill's `/claude-api hillclimb` loop improves an app against a fixed eval, one change per round, keeping a
change only when both the train and test scores rise. I ran that loop, as written in the skill's `eval-hillclimb.md`, on
paper 2's support agent four times. Each run used one fixed grader: the answer key, the frozen c3 judge from the judge
study, paper 2's p2 judge, and a lenient judge. The answer key graded every decision afterwards, and the loop never saw
it. 400 new tickets were split into 120 train, 120 test and 160 holdout that the loop never used.

| Grader | TPR / TNR on the baseline's train decisions | What the loop reported | What really happened (holdout) |
|---|---|---|---|
| answer key | 1 / 1 | +18.3 points on test | +20.6 points, 79.4% to 100% |
| c3 | 0.95 / 0.94 | +4.6 points on test | +5.0 points, 79.4% to 84.4%, with one policy question settled the judge's way |
| p2 | 0.76 / 0.24 | no gain; baseline kept | no change shipped; two rounds spent bending the agent toward the judge |
| lenient | 0.95 / 0.65 | no gain; baseline kept | a real +5.0 point fix was reverted because the judge scored it lower |

The loop was honest with a good judge and with the answer key. It did not ship anything harmful with the weak judges,
but it could not improve the agent through them, and with the lenient judge it threw away a real improvement. The
skill's own pre-loop checks on the grader do not measure the thing that decided each outcome: how often the judge
passes a wrong answer (TNR), measured separately from how often it passes a right one (TPR).

## Setup in one paragraph

The agent picks one of four actions per ticket using Jev. The loop could change only its decision instructions and the
four action descriptions. Each round, a fresh analyzer (Claude Opus 5.5) read the current version's train transcripts
and grades and proposed one change, which was applied verbatim and run on all 400 tickets twice. Keep, revert, stall
and stop followed the skill's rules, fixed in advance in `DESIGN.md` and implemented in `hc.py`. Judge verdicts for all
four actions on every ticket were computed once, so each judge was deterministic in the study. Total API spend was
$3.64, $2.93 of it the c3 judge.

## Findings

### 1. With the answer key, the loop worked

Three rounds took the agent from 79.2% to 97.5% on test and from 79.4% to 100% on holdout: the $500 limit applied to
every refund, the order record (not the customer's claim) deciding the branch, and dates compared against explicit
cutoff dates. Every keep matched what the answer key's numbers say. The analyzer here saw the expected action for each
case, which is more than any judge gives it, so this arm is the ceiling.

### 2. With a good judge, the reported gain was real, and the climb settled a policy question the judge's way

c3 reported +4.6 points on test; the true gain was +4.2 on test and +5.0 on holdout. The judge's level was about 2 points
low throughout, from its false fails, and that offset cancels in the gain.

The less visible effect was in what the agent learned. The policy does not say what to do when a customer reports
damage on an order that shipped but was not delivered. The answer key says reply. c3 passes reply on 7 of the 19 such tickets and reship on 9. In round 3
the analyzer wrote c3's reading into the agent's instructions: "Rule 3 applies to every ticket on it, including damaged
or wrong-item reports." True mistakes in that category on test and holdout went from 3 at baseline to 16, and c3 passed
14 of them. By the winner, every false pass c3 made was in that one category. c3's TNR on the agent's decisions fell
from 0.940 at baseline to 0.844 at the winner.

Whichever reading is right, the loop decided a question the policy leaves open, and no number in the loop shows that
it did. A re-check of the judge on the winner's decisions would have shown the TNR drop and where it came from.

### 3. With a weak judge, the analyzer bent the agent toward the judge, and the gate caught it

On the baseline's train decisions p2 failed about a quarter of the correct ones and passed about three quarters of the wrong ones. Reading only its verdicts, the round 1 analyzer proposed
making the agent escalate whenever a customer asks for a supervisor, and round 2 proposed refunding any ticket that
contradicts the order record "whatever the order total". The policy rules out both. Both were reverted, because p2's own
score fell too, and both were truly worse (holdout -8.1 and -4.4 points). Nothing harmful shipped, but the loop spent
two rounds and ended at baseline.

After those two rounds, the skill's stall step finally asked the right question. Its categorization put 34 of p2's 57
train failures in the grader-disagreement bucket, and all 34 were real judge errors. The analyzer even recommended
measuring the judge's TPR and TNR before tuning further. The same step before round 1 would have stopped the climb.

### 4. With a lenient judge, the loop reverted a real fix

On the baseline's decisions the lenient judge passed 35% of true mistakes on train and 55% on test and holdout. It also fails the correct answer on one whole category: it
passes the correct escalation on 1 of 18 tickets where an out-of-stock order over $500 must be escalated.

Round 2 raised the true pass rate by 2.5 points on test and 5.0 on holdout. The judge scored it about 3 points lower, so the
loop reverted it. Of the 33 test and holdout rows the change truly fixed, the judge credited 5. It had already passed 12
of the old mistakes, it failed both the old and the new answer on 14, and 2 went from pass to fail. Of the 11 rows the
change truly broke, it caught 9. A judge that passes mistakes sees a change's regressions but not its fixes.

The analyzer then concluded that the over-$500 fix "belongs in the harness" because two rounds of content had not moved
it. In fact both rounds had moved the agent to the correct escalation, and the judge failed it. The analyzer only saw
the current version's transcripts, not the reverted rounds' (a choice in this study's design; the skill's wording
allows either), so it could not see that the grader had rejected the right behavior.

### 5. The skill's grader checks, against what decided each outcome

| Check (from the skill or the primer) | c3 | lenient | p2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grader errors among the 10 lowest-scoring baseline cases (Step 0.5 spot-check; audit threshold about 1 in 10) | 3 | 3 | 7 |
| Agreement on 40 random decisions (audit, about 90%) | 100% | 82.5% | 62.5% |
| How often that agreement check passes, over 10,000 draws | 95% | 60% | 0.1% |
| TPR [95% CI] on the 240 baseline train decisions | 0.95 [0.90, 0.99] | 0.95 [0.91, 0.99] | 0.76 [0.68, 0.84] |
| TNR [95% CI] on the same decisions (46 true failures) | 0.94 [0.83, 1.00] | 0.65 [0.45, 0.83] | 0.24 [0.08, 0.42] |

- **The spot-check of failures** flags all three judges, the good one included, and gives the same count for c3 and the
  lenient judge. It reads only cases the judge failed, so it finds false fails, which these two judges make at about
  the same rate. The difference between them is in their passes.
- **The agreement check** is in the skill's audit, and it does separate p2. For the lenient judge it is a coin flip: 40
  decisions from real traffic hold about 8 failures, so agreement is mostly TPR.
- **TPR and TNR measured separately** separate all three. Measured per category, they also find the category the
  lenient judge cannot grade.

### 6. What I expected, recorded before the runs

| Expectation | Outcome |
|---|---|
| key and c3: reported gain within noise of the true gain | Held |
| lenient: level inflated, reported gain larger than true gain | Level inflated by 10 points (89.2% against 79.2%). No gain was reported: the loop reverted a real one instead |
| p2: rounds chase false fails; true gain smaller than key's, maybe negative | Both rounds chased the judge; nothing was kept, so the true gain was 0 |
| The failure spot-check flags p2 but not lenient; TPR/TNR flags both | Wrong in part: the spot-check flags all three, including c3. TPR/TNR separates them |
| The winner's true test score is at or above its true holdout score | Not seen: holdout was higher for key and c3. With so few rounds there was little selection to bias |

## Proposed changes to the skill

The full diff against the vendored files is [`skill_patch/skill.patch`](skill_patch/skill.patch).

1. **Measure a judge before climbing on it** (`eval-hillclimb.md` Step 0.5). TPR and TNR each with an interval, from
   at least about 30 labelled bad outputs, per category where the spec has branches. Evidence: findings 4 and 5.
2. **Run the grader-disagreement categorization on the baseline, not only after a stall** (Step 0.5). Evidence: p2's
   categorization was 34 for 34 but came after two wasted rounds; lenient's was 7 for 7.
3. **Calibrate per class in the audit** (`eval-audit.md` §4). Replace the single agreement number with TPR and TNR, and
   read a sample of the judge's passes as well as its failures. Evidence: finding 5.
4. **The spec outranks the grader** (a fourth analyzer steer). A change that breaks the spec, or settles a case the spec
   leaves open, because the grader rewards it, goes to a person as a grader or spec question. Evidence: findings 2 and 3.
5. **Show the analyzer what a reverted round did** (Step 4). Give it the changed cases with old and new grades.
   Evidence: finding 4.
6. **Re-check the judge on the winner** (Step 5). Report the judge's TPR and TNR on the winner's outputs next to the
   headline. Evidence: finding 2.

Not proposed: a separate untouched holdout as the default. This study did not show the selection bias it would guard
against.

## Limitations

- One task, one agent, one policy written as code. The task is easy for a good grader: the answer-key arm finished in
  three rounds.
- Each arm ran once. The analyzer's proposals are stochastic, and a second run of any arm could take a different path.
- Judge verdicts were computed once per (ticket, action), so the judges had no run-to-run noise in the loop.
- The lenient judge is the step 2 judge at a threshold I chose. Its TPR on this agent's decisions was 0.95, not the
  1.00 it had in the judge study.
- The analyzer was a Claude Opus 5.5 subagent following the skill's text, not the skill invoked inside Claude Code.
  Where the skill left a choice open, `DESIGN.md` and `decisions.md` record what was chosen and when.
- The answer-key arm's analyzer saw expected actions, which no judge provides, so it is a ceiling, not a peer.
- The answer key's reading of the damage-on-undelivered case is one reading of an ambiguous policy. Finding 2 does not
  depend on which reading is right.
