# Hill climbing against an LLM judge: what the hillclimb skill sees and misses

Bryan Falkowski · October 2026 · Design: [`DESIGN.md`](DESIGN.md) · Every number: [`RESULTS.md`](RESULTS.md), generated by `analyze.py`

*Working notes, not yet a post. Every number was checked independently against the raw data; the corrections from
that check are folded in.*

## Summary

The `claude-api` skill's `/claude-api hillclimb` loop improves an app against a fixed eval, one change per round,
keeping a change only when both the train and test scores rise. I ran that loop, as written in the skill's
`eval-hillclimb.md`, on paper 2's support agent four times. Each run used one fixed grader: the answer key, the frozen
c3 judge from the judge study, paper 2's p2 judge, and a lenient judge. In the three judge arms the loop never saw the
answer key; afterwards it graded every decision. 400 new tickets were split into 120 train, 120 test and 160 holdout
that the loop never used.

| Grader | TPR / TNR on the baseline's train decisions | What the loop reported | What really happened (holdout) |
|---|---|---|---|
| answer key | 1 / 1 | +18.3 points on test | +20.6 points, 79.4% to 100% |
| c3 | 0.95 / 0.93 | +4.6 points on test, within noise | +5.0 points, 79.4% to 84.4%, within noise; one open policy question settled the judge's way |
| p2 | 0.76 / 0.24 | no gain; baseline kept | nothing shipped; two rounds spent bending the agent toward the judge |
| lenient | 0.95 / 0.65 | no gain; baseline kept | a +5.0 point improvement was reverted because the judge scored it lower |

With the answer key the loop worked, and with c3 its reported gain matched the true one. With the two weaker judges it
did not ship anything harmful, but it could not improve the agent through them, and with the lenient judge it threw
away a real improvement. The skill's pre-loop checks on the grader do not measure what decided each outcome: how often
the judge passes a wrong answer (TNR) and fails a right one (TPR), measured separately and per kind of case.

**Repeat runs (decisions.md, entry 7).** The c3 and lenient arms ran twice more from the same baseline, verdicts and
rules. c3 kept changes in all three runs: true holdout gains +5.0, +6.9 and +7.8 points, against grader holdout gains of
+6.6, +11.6 and +9.4. Every run kept the $500 fix in round 1, then a change that sent the damage_open tickets to rule 3;
across the seven c3 rounds the judge credited all 43 broken damage_open decisions as fixes, and its TNR on test and
holdout mistakes fell from 0.94 to 0.84, 0.74 and 0.84. The lenient arm reverted all six of its rounds; each applied the
$500 fix (+2.5 holdout on those tickets every time), its J on fixes was −0.08 to 0.00, and every run ended with the
analyzer concluding the fix had failed. The "label 20 changed decisions" check held at 82% or more in 17 of 18 rounds;
the exception (c3-r2 v2, net +8 among 86 changed) was 64%.

## Setup in one paragraph

The agent picks one of four actions per ticket using Jev. The loop could change only its decision instructions and the
four action descriptions. Each round, a fresh analyzer (Claude Opus 5.5) read the current version's train transcripts
and grades and proposed one change, which was applied verbatim and run on all 400 tickets twice. Keep, revert, stall
and stop followed the skill's rules as fixed in `DESIGN.md`, plus two decisions made during the study and recorded in
`decisions.md` when they were made: analyzers after a stall see its categorization (3), and a round with no proposal
ends the arm (5), which is how three of the four arms ended. Judge verdicts for all four actions on every ticket were
computed once, so each judge was deterministic in the study. API spend on the repository's keys was $3.89, $2.93 of it
the c3 judge and $0.26 the repeat runs; the analyzers ran in this session and are not in that figure.

## Findings

### 1. With the answer key, the loop worked

Three rounds took the agent from 79.2% to 97.5% on test and from 79.4% to 100% on holdout: the $500 limit applied to
every refund, the order record (not the customer's claim) deciding the branch, and dates compared against explicit
cutoff dates. Every keep matched what the answer key's numbers say. The analyzer here saw the expected action for each
case, which is more than any judge gives it, so this arm is a ceiling, not a peer.

### 2. With a good judge, the reported gain matched the true one, and the climb settled an open question the judge's way

c3 reported +4.6 points on test; the true gain was +4.2 on test and +5.0 on holdout. All three are inside the noise
(the paired intervals include zero), and the reported and true gains agree. c3's level was about 2 points low at
baseline and at the winner, from its false fails.

The less visible effect was in what the agent learned. When a customer reports damage on an order that is Lost, or
shipped more than 14 days ago and not delivered, the policy text does not say which rule applies. The answer key says
reply. On all 13 such tickets c3 passes the action rule 3 would give (reship, or refund or escalate when out of stock),
and it passes reply on 1. In round 3 the analyzer wrote c3's reading into the agent's instructions: "Rule 3 applies to
every ticket on it, including damaged or wrong-item reports and tickets that only ask for a status update: reship if
stock is above 0, otherwise refund." Answer-key mistakes on those tickets in test and holdout went from 2 at baseline to
14 at the winner, and c3 passed all 14. At the winner, every false pass c3 made was in that category. c3's TNR on the
agent's test and holdout decisions went from 0.940 at baseline to 0.844 at the winner (109 of 116 against 76 of 90).
The two reps of a ticket are not independent; with one rep per ticket it is 53 of 57 against 38 of 45 (p = 0.2), so
this is suggestive, not conclusive.

Whichever reading is right, the loop decided a question the policy leaves open, and nothing in the loop's numbers shows
that it did. Re-checking the judge on the winner's decisions would have shown where its remaining errors are.

### 3. With a weak judge, the analyzer bent the agent toward the judge, and the gate caught it

On the baseline's train decisions p2 failed about a quarter of the correct ones and passed about three quarters of the
wrong ones. Reading only its verdicts, the round 1 analyzer made the agent escalate whenever a customer asks for a
supervisor or the ticket contradicts the record, which the policy's numbered rules don't allow. Round 2 made it refund
every ticket that contradicts the record "whatever the order total", which overrides the $500 limit. Both changes also
decided the open cases (damage on an undelivered order, or "never came" on an order the record shows Delivered)
differently from the answer key.

Both lowered p2's own score and were reverted, so nothing harmful shipped, but the loop spent two rounds and ended at
baseline. Under the answer key, round 1 would have cost 8.1 points on holdout: 5.9 on the open cases and 2.2 on cases the
numbered rules decide. Round 2 would have cost 4.4, all of it from the open cases, so that loss depends on the answer
key's reading.

After those two rounds the skill's stall step asked the right question. Its categorization put 34 of p2's 57 train
failures in the grader-disagreement bucket. All 34 were real judge errors, out of 46 real false fails. The analyzer
recommended measuring the judge's TPR and TNR before tuning further. Run before round 1, the same step could have
stopped the climb, though this categorization also drew on what rounds 1 and 2 had shown.

### 4. With a lenient judge, the loop reverted a real improvement

On the baseline's decisions the lenient judge passed 35% of true mistakes on train and 55% on test and holdout. It also
fails the correct answer for one whole category: it passes the correct escalation on 1 of the 18 tickets where an
out-of-stock order over $500 must be escalated.

Round 2 raised the true pass rate by 2.5 points on test and 5.0 on holdout. The judge scored it about 3 points lower, so
the loop reverted it. Of the 33 test and holdout rows the change truly fixed, the judge credited 5. Two causes, about
equally large, hid the rest:

- **Its TNR.** It had already passed the old mistake on 12 rows, so fixing it earned nothing.
- **Its TPR on one category.** It failed the new, correct answer on 16 rows (14 failed before and after, and 2 went from
  pass to fail). All 16 are out-of-stock escalations over $500.

Of the 11 rows the change truly broke, it caught 9. A judge like this sees a change's regressions but not most of its
fixes.

The analyzer then concluded that the over-$500 fix "belongs in the harness" because two rounds of content had not moved
it. Both rounds had in fact moved the agent to the correct escalation, and the judge failed it. The analyzer saw only the
current version's transcripts, so it could not see that. That was this study's choice, and it departs from the skill's
text, which hands the analyzer "the previous round's" traces, the reverted round's after a revert (`decisions.md`, 6).

### 5. The skill's grader checks, against what decided each outcome

| Check | c3 | lenient | p2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grader errors among the 10 lowest-scoring baseline train cases (Step 0.5 spot-check; the audit's threshold is about 1 in 10) | 2.0 on average; flags 69% of the time | 2.2; flags 79% | 8.2; flags always |
| Same, with ties broken by the judge's own score | 3 | 0 | 7 |
| Agreement on 40 random decisions (the audit's check, about 90%) | 100% | 82.5% | 62.5% |
| How often that agreement check passes, over 10,000 draws | 95% | 60% | under 0.1% |
| TPR [95% CI] on the 240 baseline train decisions | 0.95 [0.90, 0.99] | 0.95 [0.91, 0.99] | 0.76 [0.68, 0.84] |
| TNR [95% CI] on the same decisions (46 true failures) | 0.93 [0.83, 1.00] | 0.65 [0.45, 0.83] | 0.24 [0.08, 0.42] |

- **The spot-check of failures** cannot tell c3 from the lenient judge. Most cases tie at a score of 0, so which ten get
  read is arbitrary. With random tie-breaks it flags c3 about as often as the lenient judge. Read in order of the
  judge's own score, the lenient judge's ten lowest hold no grader errors at all. The check reads only cases the judge
  failed, so it finds false fails. These two judges make false fails at about the same rate and differ in their passes.
- **The agreement check** separates p2. For the lenient judge it is close to a coin flip. 40 decisions from real traffic
  hold about 8 failures, so agreement mostly measures TPR.
- **TPR and TNR measured separately** separate the three on these 240 decisions. Two cautions apply. First, with 46
  failures even c3's TNR interval reaches down to 0.83. Second, two of c3's three spot-check errors, and 8 of its 10
  train false fails, are in the open category from finding 2. Without those tickets c3's rates are 0.99 and 0.98, so
  its numbers here depend on the answer key's reading.
- **Per category, the baseline alone can't show the lenient judge's worst gap.** At baseline the agent never chose the
  correct escalation for the over-$500 out-of-stock tickets, so there were no correct outputs there to grade. Running
  the correct action through the judge per category, as the audit's oracle check does for the whole eval, finds it: 1
  of 18.

No gain was reported; the loop reverted a real one instead. The TNR part can't be tested, because the winner is the baseline |
| p2: rounds chase false fails; true gain smaller than key's, maybe negative | Both rounds chased the judge. Nothing was kept, so the true gain was 0 |

| The winner's true test score is at or above its true holdout score | Not seen: holdout was higher for key and c3. With so few rounds there was little selection to bias |

## Proposed changes to the skill

The diff against the vendored files is [`skill_patch/skill.patch`](skill_patch/skill.patch).

1. **Measure a judge before climbing on it** (`eval-hillclimb.md` Step 0.5). TPR and TNR each with an interval, from
   at least about 30 labelled bad outputs and labelled good outputs in each of the spec's main categories. Where the app
   never produces a correct output for a category, grade a reference one. Evidence: findings 4 and 5.
2. **Run the grader-disagreement categorization on the baseline, not only after a stall** (Step 0.5), as a screen next to
   the labelled check, not instead of it. Evidence: it was right on all 34 of its calls for p2 and all 7 for the lenient
   judge. For c3, 2 of its 6 calls were right, so it can't certify a good judge on its own.
3. **Calibrate per class in the audit** (`eval-audit.md` §4). Replace the single agreement number with TPR and TNR, and
   read a sample of the judge's passes as well as its failures. Evidence: finding 5.
4. **The spec outranks the grader** (a fourth analyzer steer). A change that breaks the spec, or settles a case the spec
   leaves open, because the grader rewards it, goes to a person as a grader or spec question. Evidence: findings 2 and 3.
5. **After a revert, say exactly what the analyzer sees** (Step 4). The skill's text already points the analyzer at the
   previous round's traces. The change makes that explicit, adds the changed cases' old and new grades, and says why.
   Evidence: finding 4, where this study departed from the text and the analyzer misread the cause.
6. **Re-check the judge on the winner** (Step 5). Report the judge's TPR and TNR on the winner's outputs next to the
   headline. Evidence: finding 2, which is suggestive rather than conclusive.

Not proposed: a separate untouched holdout as the default. This study did not show the selection bias it would guard
against.

## Limitations

- One task, one agent, one policy written as code. The task is easy for a good grader: the answer-key arm finished in
  three rounds.
- Each arm ran once. The analyzer's proposals are stochastic, and a second run of any arm could take a different path.
- Judge verdicts were computed once per (ticket, action), so the judges had no run-to-run noise in the loop.
- The lenient judge is the step 2 judge at a threshold I chose. Its TPR on this agent's baseline decisions was 0.95 on
  train and 0.97 overall, not the 1.00 it had in the judge study.
- The analyzer was a Claude Opus 5.5 subagent following the skill's text, not the skill invoked inside Claude Code. It
  saw the current version's traces, where the skill's text would give it the reverted round's after a revert.
- The answer-key arm's analyzer saw expected actions, which no judge provides.
- The answer key's reading of the open damage case is one reading of an ambiguous policy. Finding 2 does not depend on
  which reading is right. Round 2's loss in finding 3 does, and so do c3's numbers in finding 5.
