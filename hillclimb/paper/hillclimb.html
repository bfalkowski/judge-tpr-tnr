Can You Trust Your Hill Climb?

Applying TPR and TNR to a Keep-or-Revert Loop

Bryan Falkowski · October 2026 · Code and data: github.com/bfalkowski/judge-tpr-tnr (hillclimb/)

Third draft: the second draft, revised after an external review, plus two repeat runs each of the calibrated and lenient climbs. Every number comes from hillclimb/analyze.py and is listed in hillclimb/RESULTS.md.

Abstract. Hill climbing improves an app one change at a time: run the eval, keep the change if the score rises, revert it if not. When an LLM judge grades the eval, every keep or revert compares two judge pass rates. The primer on TPR and TNR showed that a judge's pass rate mixes in two kinds of mistake. This paper asks what those mistakes do to a hill climb. I ran the same loop on a support agent from my earlier work against four fixed graders: the answer key, and three judges whose TPR and TNR I had measured. I ran the calibrated and lenient climbs three times each. The answer key graded every decision afterwards. With the answer key, the climb raised the agent's pass rate on untouched holdout tickets from 79% to 100%. With a calibrated judge, all three climbs made true gains of 5 to 8 points on holdout, but all three settled an open policy question the judge's way, and the share of the agent's mistakes the judge passed rose from 6% to between 16% and 26%. With a lenient judge, all three climbs reverted every change they tried, though every one truly fixed more decisions than it broke, because on balance the judge credited none of the fixes. With a weak judge, nothing the loop tried could register. If a judge's TPR and TNR were the same for every output, it would show any change at J = TPR + TNR − 1 times its true size. They are not, and the climbs show why that matters: a judge's quality on the baseline does not tell you how it grades the particular changes a loop explores. The last sections show how to measure that before, during and after a climb.

1.The question

Hill climbing is a common way to improve a prompt or an agent. Each round you make one change, run the eval, and keep the change only if the score goes up. Anthropic's guide for doing this with Claude splits the cases in two: the analyzer that proposes changes reads only one part, and a change is kept only when the scores on both parts rise (Martin 2026). That guards against overfitting to the cases you read.

It does not guard against the grader. When the grader is an LLM judge, "the score went up" means "the judge passed more outputs." The primer showed what a judge's pass rate is made of (Falkowski 2026a):

pass rate = TPR × θ + (1 − TNR) × (1 − θ)

where θ is the true share of good outputs. Every keep or revert in a hill climb compares two of these numbers.

So what does a change do to the judge's pass rate? Suppose a change fixes F outputs that were wrong and breaks B outputs that were right, out of n. Before the change, the judge passed each of the F wrong outputs with probability 1 − TNR. After it, the judge passes each fixed output with probability TPR. On average the judge credits each fix with

TPR − (1 − TNR) = TPR + TNR − 1

and by the same argument it charges each break the same amount. TPR + TNR − 1 is Youden's J (Youden 1950), the number the primer used to show why a weak judge can't be corrected. If the judge's TPR and TNR are the same for every output, which I'll call the uniform-error model, then

expected observed change = J × true change, where true change = (F − B) / n.

Under this model a judge shrinks every real improvement by J. A judge with J = 0.88 shows a 10-point gain as about 8.8 points, a judge with J = 0.6 shows it as 6, and a judge with J = 0 shows nothing. A judge with negative J favors worse outputs. It also gives a rule of thumb: when J is clearly above zero, the smallest true gain the loop can tell from its noise is about the noise divided by J.

The uniform-error model is the starting point, not the result. A change in a hill climb usually touches a few kinds of output, and a judge's rates differ by kind. Each fixed output adds its own J, the judge's TPR on its new answer minus its false-pass rate on the old one, and each broken output subtracts its own:

expected observed change = (sum of J over the fixed outputs − sum of J over the broken outputs) / n.

So the net count F − B no longer decides what the judge sees. The mix of fixes and breaks does. A change can be a real improvement and still lower the judge's score, if the outputs it fixes are ones the judge grades poorly. I'll call the average J over a change's fixes its "J on fixes," and the average over its breaks its "J on breaks."

That reframes the question. It is not only whether a judge is accurate, but whether it is reliable enough to steer an optimization. Those are different. The loop changes what the agent produces, that changes where the judge's errors fall, and that shapes the next round. The rest of this paper shows what that does to a real climb, and how to measure it.

2.Setup

The agent. The support agent from my run-validity paper (Falkowski 2026b), imported unchanged. For each ticket it looks up the order and stock and asks Jev, a small decision model from TypeSafe, which of four actions the five-rule support policy calls for: refund, reship, escalate, or reply. The climb could change only two things: the decision instructions sent with every question, and the descriptions of the four actions. The policy text, the fields the agent sees, the model and the code were off-limits.

The answer key. The policy written as code, as in my run-validity paper. Every decision has a known correct action.

Tickets. 400 new tickets from the same generator as the labelled tickets I used to refine the judges below, split at random within each customer framing into three sets:

Set Tickets Role in the loop
Train 120 The analyzer reads these transcripts to propose changes
Validation 120 Scored every round; a change is kept only if its score here rises. The guide calls this set "test"
Holdout 160 Never used by the loop; scored only afterwards

Because the validation set decides which changes survive, it is part of the optimization. The holdout set is the independent evidence of whether a gain is real. Each version of the agent ran every ticket twice, so a set's pass rate is a mean over two decisions per ticket, 240 on validation.

The loop. I followed Anthropic's published hill-climbing guide as written: the eval-hillclimb.md file of the claude-api skill (Anthropic 2026), vendored in the repo at a fixed commit. Each round, a fresh analyzer (Claude Opus 5.5) read the current version's train transcripts and grades and proposed one change, which was applied word for word. A change was kept when its train and validation scores both rose. When validation rose and train did not, the change was run once more and kept only if validation rose again. After two rounds without a real rise, the loop ran one round that sorted the remaining failures by cause, and it stopped after three such rounds or when the analyzer had nothing to propose. The study's design and every decision made during it are recorded in the repo before the results they could affect, and the branch hillclimb-history keeps the commit-by-commit record with each round's timestamp. After the first four climbs, I ran the calibrated and lenient climbs twice more, from the same baseline with the same judge verdicts and rules. Only the analyzer's proposals, and so what the loop kept, could differ between runs.

The graders. Each climb used one fixed grader for every round. I measured each judge on the baseline's 240 train decisions, 46 of which were wrong:

Grader What it is TPR TNR J
answer key the policy as code 1.00 1.00 1.00
calibrated a judge (Claude Haiku 4.5) whose prompt I refined against labelled tickets from earlier batches; its code is in the repo 0.95 0.93 0.88
lenient an earlier judge from that refinement (Jev), at a low threshold 0.95 0.65 0.61
weak the judge from my run-validity paper (Jev, one sentence) 0.76 0.24 0.00

A judge's input is fully set by the ticket, order, stock and action, so I ran each judge once on all four actions of every ticket before the climbs started. The loop looked up the verdict for whatever action the agent chose. Total API spend was $3.89, of which $2.93 was the calibrated judge's verdicts and $0.26 the repeat runs. In the repo the three judges are c3, j2 at threshold 0.3, and p2.

Noise. The guide's rule of thumb for a pass rate is 1/√(cases × runs), about 6.5 points here. That treats all 240 validation decisions as independent, and it is for one pass rate, not a comparison. I measured two kinds of noise directly. Re-running the same version changed very little: between the baseline's two runs, 0 to 2 of the 120 validation tickets got a different grade, depending on the grader, which is about 1 to 2 points of noise for comparing two versions. The larger term is which tickets happen to be in the set. Paired 95% intervals on each round's change, resampling tickets, had half-widths of about 3 to 8 points. I use the paired intervals below.

The next three sections take the cases in turn.

4.A judge that passes mistakes can't see fixes

The lenient judge's J on the baseline was 0.61. On the decisions its first run's two rounds fixed, its J was 0.00.

Round 2 made the $500 refund limit apply to every refund, which the policy requires. It fixed 36 decisions and broke 10. The judge charged 7 of the 10 breaks, and it credited the 36 fixes with nothing. Four went from fail to pass and four from pass to fail. Five it had passed before and after, because it had already passed the old mistake. Twenty-three it failed before and after. That last group explains most of it: on tickets where an out-of-stock order over $500 has to be escalated, this judge passes the correct escalation on 1 of 18. Its TPR on that kind of decision is close to zero. Twenty-six of the 27 fixed decisions it failed after the change are those escalations.

So the change was a real improvement, and the judge scored it as a loss. The loop reverted it, correctly by its own rule. Round 1 tried the same fix, and the judge credited none of its 36 fixes either.

The repeat runs did the same thing. Every one of the six lenient rounds across the three runs made the $500 limit apply to the out-of-stock refund, and the over-$500 out-of-stock tickets alone gained 2.5 points on holdout each time. The calibrated judge kept the same fix in round 1 of all three of its runs. In all six lenient rounds, the judge's J on fixes was between −0.08 and 0.00, it reported a validation drop of 2.5 to 4.6 points with an interval that excluded zero, and the loop reverted. All six truly fixed more decisions than they broke, and the same keep rule applied to answer-key scores would have kept five of them. The best reverted round in each run would have raised holdout by 5.0, 3.8 and 9.1 points.

Each run then ended the same way. The analyzer read the history, saw two rounds aimed at the $500 limit that the judge had scored as losses, and proposed nothing. Run 1's analyzer described the over-limit refunds as a behavior "that two artifact rounds already failed to move," and run 3's said the same behavior "has already resisted both in-scope levers." On the over-$500 out-of-stock tickets the fix had worked every time. The judge's blind spot became the analyzer's conclusion about the agent.

Two different gaps hid the fixes, and they need different repairs. Low TNR on the old mistakes means the judge already passed them, so fixing them earns nothing. Low TPR on the new correct answers means the judge fails them, so fixing them can even cost. Neither shows in the judge's average, and the second can't be measured on the baseline at all, because at baseline the agent never produced the correct escalation. Grading a correct answer for each kind of ticket finds it.

5.The climb moves mistakes to where the judge can't see them

The calibrated judge is a good one, with J = 0.88 on the baseline. Its first climb reported +4.6 points on validation, and the true gain was +4.2 on validation and +5.0 on holdout.

In that run its J on breaks was much lower than its overall J: 0.50, 0.41 and 0.10 in the three rounds. Most of the breaks it missed were on one kind of ticket. When a customer reports damage on an order that is Lost, or that shipped more than 14 days ago and was never delivered, the policy text doesn't say which rule applies. The answer key says reply. The calibrated judge passes the action rule 3 would give (reship, or refund or escalate when out of stock) on all 13 such tickets, and it passes reply on 1.

In round 3 the analyzer, reading the judge's verdicts, wrote the judge's reading into the agent's instructions: "Rule 3 applies to every ticket on it, including damaged or wrong-item reports and tickets that only ask for a status update: reship if stock is above 0, otherwise refund." The change broke 20 decisions. The judge charged the 11 that were ordinary mistakes and credited the 9 on those open tickets as fixes. Round 2 had done the same on a larger scale: 10 of its 46 breaks were on the open tickets, which the judge counted as fixes.

Both repeat runs did the same. Each kept the $500 fix in round 1, then kept a change that sent the open tickets to rule 3. Run 2's instructions put a shipped order more than 14 days old under "rule 3, whatever the ticket reports, including damage, a wrong item or only a request for an update." Run 3's said "reship if stock is above 0, otherwise refund, even when the ticket reports a damaged or wrong item." Across the seven calibrated rounds in the three runs, changes broke 43 decisions on the open tickets, and the judge credited all 43 as fixes. It charged 105 of the other 127 breaks.

Two related things are happening here.

The first is selection. The loop keeps changes the judge rewards, so it pulls the agent toward the judge's reading of anything the judge reads differently from the answer key. That is Goodhart's law (Goodhart 1975): once the judge's score is the target, the loop optimizes the judge's opinion along with the agent's quality.

The second is a shift in where the judge's errors fall, and this one is the empirical finding. The climb changed which mistakes the agent makes, so the judge's error rates on the baseline stopped describing it. On validation and holdout, answer-key mistakes on the open tickets went from 2 at baseline to 14 at the first run's winner, 16 at the second's and 12 at the third's, and the judge passed every one of them. Most of its false passes at each winner were on that one kind of ticket: 14 of 14, 16 of 22 and 12 of 12. Its TNR on the agent's decisions fell from 0.94 at baseline to 0.84, 0.74 and 0.84 (109 of 116 mistaken decisions failed at baseline, against 76 of 90, 62 of 84 and 62 of 74). The two runs of a ticket are not independent, so I also counted one run per ticket: 53 of 57 at baseline, against 38 of 45, 32 of 43 and 33 of 39 at the three winners. On its own, only run 2's drop is unlikely to be chance (p = 0.02, against 0.2 and 0.3). Three runs that share a baseline are not proof, but the drop has the same direction and the same cause in each.

The argument doesn't depend on which reading is right. The point is that the loop resolved an ambiguity in the policy, resolved it the judge's way, and never showed that decision to anyone. Nothing in the loop's numbers says a policy question was settled.

6.A judge with J near zero teaches the wrong rules

The weak judge's J on the baseline was 0.00: its verdicts carried almost no information about whether a decision was right. The analyzer had no way to know that. It read the verdicts, found patterns, and wrote them into the agent.

In round 1 it made the agent escalate whenever a customer asks for a supervisor or a ticket contradicts the order record. The policy only escalates in two situations, and a customer's request is not one of them. In round 2 it made the agent refund any ticket that contradicts the record "whatever the order total", which skips the $500 limit. Both changes also decided two kinds of ticket the policy leaves open differently from the answer key: the damage reports from Section 5, and "never came" on an order the record shows Delivered. Both lowered the weak judge's own score, so both were reverted, and nothing harmful shipped. Under the answer key, round 1 would have cost 8.1 points on holdout: 5.9 on those open tickets and 2.2 on tickets the numbered rules decide. Round 2 would have cost 4.4, all of it from the open tickets, so that loss depends on the answer key's reading.

After two wasted rounds, the loop's stall step asked the right question. It sorted the remaining train failures by cause and put 34 of the weak judge's 57 in a "grader disagreement" bucket. All 34 were real judge errors. It recommended measuring the judge's TPR and TNR before tuning further. That was the first step the climb needed, and it came last.

7.Applying TPR and TNR to a hill climb

The climbs suggest a procedure. Some steps are demonstrated here. Others are hypotheses this study supports but does not establish, and I mark them.

Before the climb: measure TPR, TNR and J. Demonstrated here. Label decisions from the baseline, with at least about 30 wrong ones, and measure TPR and TNR with intervals. If J is not clearly above zero, the right move is to improve or replace the judge. More cases or runs won't help, because the judge's score barely moves with true quality. If J is clearly positive, divide the noise of a paired comparison by J for a rough sense of the smallest true gain the loop can register, and compare it with the improvement you hope to find. That threshold is a rule of thumb under the uniform-error model, not a guarantee, because the J that matters is the one on the outputs a change touches.

Two common checks did not separate these judges. Reading the lowest-scoring cases finds false fails, the judge failing correct outputs. The calibrated and lenient judges make those at about the same rate, and they differ in what they pass. A random sample of 40 decisions from real traffic holds about 8 wrong ones, so agreement on it mostly measures TPR:

Check on the baseline's train decisions calibrated lenient weak
Grader errors among the 10 lowest-scoring cases, ties broken at random (average over 10,000 draws) 2.0 2.2 8.2
How often 40 random decisions reach 90% agreement (10,000 draws) 95% 60% under 0.1%
TPR 0.95 0.95 0.76
TNR 0.93 0.65 0.24

Most of the lowest-scoring cases tie at a score of 0, so which ten get read is arbitrary. Read in order of the lenient judge's own score, its ten lowest hold no grader errors at all.

Before the climb: measure the judge by kind of output. Demonstrated here. A judge can be fine on average and have J near zero on one kind of output. On the baseline you often can't see that, because the app never produces the correct output there. Grade a correct output for each main branch of the spec. The lenient judge fails the correct escalation on 17 of 18 tickets. That check would have found it in minutes, before the loop spent two rounds in each of three runs on a fix it could never credit.

During the climb: label a sample of the changed outputs. Supported retrospectively, not yet tested in a loop. A round changes few outputs. Here each round changed 44 to 97 of its 480 train and validation decisions, and every keep or revert depends only on those. Labelling a small random sample of them estimates the true change without trusting the judge. To check how small, I took each round's changed decisions and computed the chance that k of them, drawn at random without replacement and labelled with the answer key, give fixes minus breaks with the same sign as the count over all of them. Across the 18 rounds of all the runs, at k = 20 that chance was at least 81% in 17 rounds, and 98% or more in 9. The exception was the calibrated judge's second round in run 2, which fixed 45 decisions and broke 37. Its net of +8 among 86 changed decisions is hard to sign from a sample: 20 labels get it right 64% of the time, and 30 labels 70%. At k = 30 every other round was 91% or higher. These are rounds of one task, so they show the idea can work, not that 20 is enough in general. For this task, 20 decisions is a few minutes of review. The same labels show where J on fixes and J on breaks differ, which is how the lenient judge's reverts and the calibrated judge's round 2 would have been caught.

During the climb: treat a change that breaks the spec as a judge problem. A hypothesis from a few examples. When the analyzer proposes something the spec rules out, or settles a case the spec leaves open, because the grader rewards it, the grader is the thing to look at. The weak judge's two rounds and the rounds in each calibrated run that sent the open tickets to rule 3 all started that way.

After the climb: measure the judge again on the winner. Repeated in three runs, significant in one. The judge's rates on the final outputs can differ from its rates on the baseline, because the climb changed which mistakes the agent makes. The calibrated judge's TNR fell in all three runs (Section 5). Re-measure TPR and TNR on a labelled sample of the winner's outputs, including outputs the judge passed, and report them next to the gain. Don't correct the winner's pass rate with the baseline's rates. With them, the three calibrated winners correct to 86.7%, 92.4% and 89.9% on holdout. The truth was 84.4%, 86.2% and 87.2%, and the raw pass rates, 83.1%, 88.1% and 85.9%, were closer each time. The correction needs rates that hold on the data it corrects.

8.Limitations

  • One task, one agent, and a policy written as code. The task is easy for a good grader: the answer-key arm was done in three rounds.
  • The calibrated and lenient climbs ran three times each, and the answer-key and weak climbs once. The repeats share the baseline, the splits and the judge verdicts, so they vary only the analyzer's proposals and what the loop kept. Three runs show which results repeat, not how often they would.
  • Judge verdicts were computed once per ticket and action, so the judges had no run-to-run noise in the loop. Real climbs have that noise on top of the effects here.
  • The lenient judge is an earlier judge from the calibrated judge's refinement, at a threshold I chose to make it lenient.
  • The analyzer was a Claude Opus 5.5 subagent following the guide's text, not the guide running inside Claude Code. It saw the current version's transcripts each round. After a reverted round, the guide's text would have given it the reverted version's, which might have helped it notice the lenient judge failing the correct escalations. Without them, all three lenient runs ended with the analyzer concluding the fix had failed.
  • The answer-key arm's analyzer saw each case's expected action, which no judge gives. That arm is a ceiling, not a peer.
  • The answer key's reading of the open tickets is one reading of an ambiguous policy. Section 5's point doesn't depend on which reading is right. The weak judge's round 2 loss does.
  • J on fixes and J on breaks are measured with the answer key, which a real climb doesn't have. Section 7's labelling step is how to estimate them without one, and I tested it only retrospectively.

9.In practice

  • Before a climb, measure the judge's TPR and TNR on labelled wrong outputs, not on a few dozen cases of real traffic, and grade a correct output for each kind of case. If J = TPR + TNR − 1 is not clearly above zero, fix the judge first.
  • A judge's quality on the baseline does not tell you how it grades the changes a loop explores. Each round, label a sample of the outputs the change touched. That is where the judge's errors decide the round.
  • Measure the judge again on the winner before you report its gain.
  • Evaluate the evaluator on the decisions the optimization actually makes, not only on a fixed dataset.

How this was made

I chose the question and the four graders. Claude (Opus 5.5) built the harness, ran the loop, wrote the analysis scripts, and drafted this paper from their output. A separate Claude agent then checked every number in the findings against the raw data, and its corrections are included. An external reviewer read the first draft, and the second draft follows that review. The repeat runs, which the reviewer ranked as the most useful addition, came after it, and a separate Claude agent checked their numbers the same way. The judges, agent runs and analysis are in the repo, with each run's data on its data branch.
